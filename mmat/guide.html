How to pass the McQuaig Mental Agility Test (MMAT)

Original guide · Updated June 2026

The MMAT is a strictly timed test — typically about 50 questions in 15 minutes, roughly 18 seconds per question. It is not academically hard; the difficulty is the clock. The winning approach is simple: move fast, never get stuck, guess on anything that isn't quick, and practise the formats until they're automatic. This guide gives you the overall game plan and a step-by-step method for every question type.

What does the Mental Agility Test actually measure?

It measures mental agility — how quickly and accurately you think, not how much you know. Questions are split across three families: verbal (vocabulary, analogies), numerical (mental arithmetic, series, word problems) and logical (deduction, patterns). Because it's heavily speeded, your score reflects throughput: how many you get right before time runs out.

That single fact — it's a speed test — drives every piece of advice below. A candidate who is 90% accurate but only reaches 20 questions usually scores lower than one who is 80% accurate across 40. Your job is to raise both dials, with speed first.

What is the best overall strategy for the MMAT?

Follow five rules: (1) answer in order but never dwell, (2) hard-cap each question at ~20 seconds, (3) never leave a blank — there's no penalty for guessing, (4) eliminate obviously wrong options first, and (5) keep a steady rhythm rather than chasing any single tough question.

  1. Triave as you go. If a question will clearly take more than ~20 seconds, mark a quick guess and move on. One hard question is never worth three easy ones.
  2. Guess everything. Scoring is the number correct, so a blank and a wrong answer cost the same. In the last 30 seconds, fill every remaining bubble.
  3. Eliminate, don't agonise. Cutting two of four options turns a guess from 25% to 50%. Elimination is faster than full solving and often enough.
  4. Protect your rhythm. Panic costs more time than any question. Breathe, keep the same steady pace, and trust the practice.
  5. Bank the easy marks. Vocabulary and simple arithmetic are the fastest points on the test — get every one of them before time pressure builds.

How to solve synonym & antonym questions

Decide the word's polarity (positive or negative) and rough meaning before you read the options, then match. For antonyms, define the word first and look for its mirror — don't be fooled by a synonym planted among the choices.

  1. Gut-check the polarity. Is the word broadly positive, negative or neutral? BENEVOLENT feels positive, so its opposite must be negative.
  2. Define it in your own words before looking down. If you can paraphrase GARRULOUS as "talks a lot," the answer jumps out.
  3. Use word roots. bene- (good), mal- (bad), -verbose/verbal (words). Roots rescue you on unfamiliar vocabulary.
  4. Spot the trap. In antonym questions, one option is usually a synonym. Cross it out deliberately.
Example: opposite of AUGMENT? Augment is positive/“increase,” so seek a negative “decrease” word → diminish.

How to solve verbal analogies

Build a precise "bridge" sentence linking the first pair, then apply the exact same sentence to the second pair. The tighter your sentence, the easier the elimination.

  1. State the relationship as a sentence. "A glove is worn on a hand." Vague bridges ("they're related") let in wrong answers.
  2. Plug in the second pair. "A ___ is worn on a head." → hat. If two options both fit, make the sentence stricter.
  3. Name the relationship type — opposite, part-to-whole, worker-to-tool, intensity (drizzle→downpour), category. Naming it speeds matching.
  4. Keep word order. If it's young→adult, your answer must be the adult, not the reverse.
Example: Cartographer : Map :: Lexicographer : ? Bridge: "makes a." A lexicographer makes a → dictionary.

How to solve "odd one out" questions

Find the shared category that covers most of the items, then remove the one that breaks it. Look past the obvious feature for a second rule when two answers seem possible.

  1. Name what unites the majority. "These are all metals…" then the non-metal is the odd one.
  2. Test a second dimension if it's close — 2-D vs 3-D shapes, element vs alloy, fruit vs vegetable.
  3. Beware the decoy. The most different-looking item isn't always the answer; trust the rule, not the vibe.
Example: Copper, Bronze, Iron, Gold → Bronze (the others are elements; bronze is an alloy).

How to solve number series questions

Compute the differences between terms first. If the differences are constant it's addition; if they grow steadily, check second differences; if the terms multiply, test a ×n (±k) rule. A few standard patterns cover most questions.

  1. Write the gaps. 3, 7, 15, 31 → gaps 4, 8, 16 (doubling) → the rule is ×2 + 1.
  2. Check second differences. If gaps themselves go 2, 4, 6, 8, the next gap is 10.
  3. Try multiply-and-tweak. When numbers grow fast, test ×2, ×3, then ±1, ±2 (e.g. 5, 8, 17, 44 is ×3 − 7).
  4. Recognise the classics: squares (1, 4, 9, 16), cubes (1, 8, 27), Fibonacci (each = sum of previous two), primes, factorials.
Example: 2, 6, 12, 20, 30, ? Gaps 4, 6, 8, 10 → next gap 12 → 42.

How to solve arithmetic & word problems fast

Estimate and round before computing exactly, and lean on percentage and ratio shortcuts. With ~18 seconds and no calculator, the goal is a confident approximation that matches exactly one option.

  1. Round, then refine. 17 × 99 ≈ 17 × 100 − 17 = 1700 − 17 = 1683. Multiplying by 9s, 11s and 99s has neat shortcuts.
  2. Make percentages friendly. 10% is "move the decimal"; build others from it (20% = ×2, 5% = half). 12.5% = one-eighth.
  3. Use "parts" for ratios. Split in 3:4 → 7 parts; one part = total ÷ 7. Most ratio questions are one division away.
  4. Watch the percentage-change trap. Up 20% then down 20% is not back to the start; multiply the factors (×1.2 × 0.8 = 0.96).
  5. Average speed ≠ average of speeds. Use total distance ÷ total time.
Example: a price rises 20% to $96. Original = 96 ÷ 1.2 = $80 (divide by the growth factor, don't subtract 20%).

How to solve letter series questions

Convert letters to their position numbers (A = 1 … Z = 26) and the puzzle becomes a number series. Watch for two interleaved sequences moving in opposite directions.

  1. Number the letters. B, D, G, K → 2, 4, 7, 11 → gaps 2, 3, 4 → next gap 5 → 16 = P.
  2. Split letter pairs. In AZ, CX, EV the first letters rise (A, C, E) while the second fall (Z, X, V).
  3. Wrap around if needed. Past Z, continue from A (it's a 26-cycle).

How to solve syllogisms & logical deduction

Treat "all / some / no" literally and, when unsure, sketch quick circles (Venn-style) or a simple order line. Accept only conclusions that must be true in every case — a statement that's merely possible is wrong.

  1. Draw the statements. "All A are B" = circle A inside B. "No B are C" = B and C separate. Read the answer off the picture.
  2. Demand certainty. If you can imagine one case where the conclusion fails, reject it. "Some flowers fade" follows; "all roses fade quickly" usually doesn't.
  3. Use the contrapositive. "If it rains, the match is off. The match was on" ⇒ it didn't rain.
  4. For ordering, build one line. Taller-than / older-than chains collapse to a single ranking you can scan.

How to solve coding, directions & blood-relation puzzles

For codes, find the letter shift (compare positions). For directions, sketch the path and track net movement. For relationships, translate one link at a time from the speaker outward.

  1. Codes: line up letters with their target. COLD → DPME is each letter +1, so WIND → XJOE.
  2. Directions: draw arrows; opposite legs cancel. East 4 then West 4 leaves only the north–south distance.
  3. Relations: rephrase step by step. "My grandfather's only son" = my father; his daughter = my sister.

A simple one-week practice plan

  1. Day 1 — baseline. Take the free 5-minute test. Note which of the three categories is weakest.
  2. Days 2–4 — drill weak spots. Use the adaptive "weak areas" round; do 2–3 short sessions a day. Review every worked answer.
  3. Days 5–6 — full timed forms. Sit a complete 15-minute form each day to build stamina and pacing.
  4. Day 7 — simulate. One clean run under strict timing, then rest. Trust your rhythm on the real day.

Frequently asked questions

How long is the McQuaig Mental Agility Test?

It's a tightly timed test — most commonly described as about 50 questions in 15 minutes, roughly 18 seconds per question. It measures speed as well as accuracy, and almost no one finishes every question.

Can you use a calculator?

No. Numerical questions are designed for mental arithmetic and estimation. Practising fast times tables, percentages and rounding pays off directly.

What is a good MMAT score?

There's no universal pass mark. Employers compare your raw score to a norm group and often set a benchmark for the specific role. Because the test is speeded, answering more questions accurately is what raises your score.

How can I practise for free?

Take the free 5-minute mental agility test on this site: 12 mixed questions with a worked answer for each. It's the quickest way to get comfortable with the time pressure and formats.

Is the test hard?

The questions themselves aren't advanced — vocabulary, school-level maths and basic logic. The difficulty is the clock, and that's exactly what practice improves.
